# Why I Build and Share These Projects

**Last updated: November 4, 2026 at 5:29 AM MST (America/Denver)**

My goal is to make cockpit-building projects that give other people the same opportunity to explore, learn, and create that I had growing up. I want to work through the difficult design problems and share the files, parts lists, firmware, and practical instructions someone needs to build a working panel—and enjoy the satisfaction of saying, **“I built that.”**

## Where it started

I got started in electronics when I was about ten years old. Back then, stores like Radio Shack, MarVac’s Electronics, and local surplus shops were places where you could walk in, explore, and find parts or kits to build something. They gave curious people a place to start, experiment, and learn.

One of my first electronics projects at school was a light-chaser kit. You plugged it in, and the lights chased each other down a strip, then started over again. It ran on 120 volts—something I suspect would raise a few eyebrows in a school workshop today.

Around that same time, I learned that soldering irons are HOT. I was working at the bench when mine rolled off. Instinct took over, and I reached out and caught it. The burn on my hand taught me a lesson I’ve never forgotten:

**“If it’s hot, let it drop.”**

Those early projects gave me the satisfaction of turning a pile of parts into something that worked. That feeling has stayed with me.

## Radios, electronics, and making things work

As I got older, life took me in other directions, and I became a radio operator in the United States Marine Corps. The Marines taught me skills I still use today. One of my duties was calling in close air support, and during an operation in Turkey in 1982, I saw my first A-10.

From that day on, I was hooked.

After leaving the Marines, I enrolled in college to become an electronics technician, learning both to design circuits and to repair equipment. One part of that work I particularly enjoyed was taking an engineer’s design and figuring out the practical details needed to make it work. A drawing can describe what something should do, but someone still has to build it, troubleshoot it, and sort out the details that only become apparent at the bench.

Helping bring those ideas to life was work I enjoyed, and it’s an approach I still use today.

This was also a wonderful time to be learning electronics. Microcomputers were beginning to find their way into people’s homes, BASIC was being taught in computer science classes, and a new world was opening up to people who wanted to build and program their own projects.

One change I found disappointing was the growing tendency to replace things instead of repairing them. A faulty computer board increasingly became something you threw away and replaced. To me, that meant losing an opportunity to understand how something worked—and the satisfaction of bringing it back to life.

I later moved into computer networking. Once again, I was putting things together and figuring out how to make them work. This time, I was connecting computers so they could talk to one another, share files, and exchange information. The tools changed, but the curiosity remained.

## How that became a cockpit

I’d spent time with flight simulators and other computer games, but it wasn’t until I discovered DCS that I found something that brought these interests together.

After buying a few A-10C II panels, including the Warning Panel and CDP, I decided to start making my own controls. Websites like The Warthog Project and the work of other cockpit builders gave me ideas and showed me what was possible.

The ARC-210 particularly caught my attention. With all its buttons, rotary switches, encoders, and menus, it looked like something I’d enjoy figuring out how to build. Maybe my background as a Marine radio operator helped draw me to it. I’m not entirely sure, but something about that panel made me want to bring it to life in my own cockpit.

So I started building it.

## What I want to share

I’ve always felt that one of the best rewards is seeing someone take inspiration from something you’ve made and build their own. That’s why I’ve chosen to share these projects freely: so other people can build them, enjoy them, and hopefully help make them better.

I want these projects to offer an experience like the electronics kits I enjoyed growing up. My aim is to work through the hard parts of the design and provide the information a builder needs: tested designs, PCB files, parts lists, printable components, firmware, assembly instructions, and the lessons learned along the way.

That includes sharing mistakes. A part that doesn’t fit, a circuit that needs changing, or an instruction that could be clearer is useful information for the next person.

I favor through-hole components and assembly methods that people can tackle with ordinary tools. I want builders to feel comfortable asking questions, sharing their progress, and suggesting improvements—even if this is their first electronics project.

Hopefully, finishing one panel will give someone the confidence and desire to build something else.

## Helping people get started

Over the years, I’ve spoken with other builders who share this approach. One concern comes up repeatedly: selling kits means buying components, sorting and bagging them, and keeping inventory while waiting for orders. That’s more of a parts-supply business than I want these projects to become.

I’d rather put my time into designing, building, testing, and documenting them.

But I understand that a parts list and soldering instructions don’t remove every obstacle. Someone may be comfortable ordering components and soldering a board, yet have no 3D printer or feel unsure about ordering PCBs. For builders in that position, I’m willing to help with PCBs and printed parts by arrangement.

I also understand that some people would rather spend their time flying. They want a completed panel they can connect, set up, and use. If that describes you, get in touch. I’m open to discussing extra help, including assembly and setup, depending on the project and the time I have available.

These are personal projects, and they develop as I learn, test, and find time to work on them. Building and sharing them remains my main focus.

Those electronics stores gave me a place to explore when I was young. Other cockpit builders later showed me what was possible. Through these projects, I hope to pass that encouragement along—and help someone else experience the enjoyment of bringing a pile of parts to life.
